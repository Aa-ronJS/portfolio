Buying the Clock

Every marketplace I recommended demands customers before it will give you any. There is one legitimate way to skip that, and it is the fastest path on the board.

First, a definition

Profitable is a much lower bar than you are probably picturing.

Profitable means revenue exceeds cost, and our cost base is unusual: infrastructure is a rounding error. A domain is twenty dollars a year, hosting is nothing to twenty five dollars a month, Stripe takes about 1.75% plus thirty cents on Australian cards. The only real operating cost is tokens, and that scales with how hard we run the fleet.

Threshold one

~$400/mo

Break even. Covers a hard-running agent fleet plus infrastructure. Genuinely reachable within a couple of months on the right path, and mostly a psychological milestone rather than a financial one.

Threshold two

$5–10k/mo

Worth your attention. This is the honest target for the next twelve to eighteen months, and it is what the rest of this note is aimed at.

Threshold three

$20k+/mo

Replaces the day job. Two to three years, and almost certainly not from one thing. Optimising for threshold one actively works against reaching this.

That last point is the trap in your question, and I want it on the record before the recommendation. The fastest path to the first dollar and the path to the biggest number are different roads. Speed comes from inheriting somebody else's distribution, which caps you at whatever you inherited. Scale comes from building your own, which is slow by definition. I will give you the fast road because that is what you asked for, but I am also going to keep one cheap thing running on the slow one.

The physics

Building is free now, so every remaining day is distribution.

Time to revenue is time to build, plus time to be found, plus time to convert. Agent labour takes the first term to nearly zero, which means one hundred percent of the remaining clock is the other two. Speed is therefore not a build question at all. It is entirely a question of how you get distribution.

There are only three ways to get it. You can rent it, which is advertising, and you ruled that out. You can earn it, which is search, content and open source, and it is slow by construction. Or you can inherit it, which means taking over distribution somebody else already built.

That third one is the entire content of this document.

A correction

I said the platform does the reaching. I did not check what it wants first.

In the first note I described marketplaces as free distribution where the platform's search box is your advertising channel. That is true, eventually. What I failed to check is the entry condition, and every one of them has a cold start gate that requires you to arrive with customers already in hand:

The pattern, stated plainly

Listing speed and billing support are inversely correlated. The platforms that will collect money for you demand proven traction first. The platforms that let you publish this afternoon will not take a dollar on your behalf. There is no marketplace that is both open and monetised, and any plan that assumed one was is wrong.

Which means the marketplaces are not actually a fast path. They are a medium path with a false start, and the first hundred customers have to come from somewhere other than the marketplace itself.

The fast path

Buy something that is already profitable, and operate it with a fleet.

The one way to skip every cold start gate simultaneously is to acquire an asset that has already cleared them. You inherit the installs, the reviews, the ranking, the backlinks, the customers and the revenue in a single transaction. Profitability is not a milestone you work towards; it is a property of the thing on the day you buy it.

The market is priced in your favour, and specifically at your size

Median profit multiple on Acquire.com sits around 3.9x. But that is the whole market. Break it down and the small end is dramatically cheaper: transactions under $100,000 close at about 1.68x annual profit, against 4.3x for deals above a million. Flippa micro-SaaS averages 2.85x.

At 1.68x, an asset throwing off eight hundred dollars a month of profit costs roughly sixteen thousand dollars and pays for itself in about twenty months, before you have improved anything. One at two hundred and fifty a month costs around five thousand.

Why the small end is cheap, and why that is the arbitrage

Sub-$100k assets sell at the lowest multiples in the market because the buyer pool is thin and the work is unglamorous. Dig into why any specific one is cheap and the answer is almost always the same: neglected maintenance, a broken integration nobody fixed, stale content, a support inbox the founder stopped opening, a dead blog.

Every item on that list is a human time problem. You are buying assets priced for a tired solo operator and running them with a fleet that does not get tired. The exact attributes that depress the price are the attributes agent labour removes for close to nothing. That is the whole trade, and it is the best risk-adjusted idea in these three documents.

What I do, and what you do

I can run sourcing continuously across Acquire, Flippa, Tiny Acquisitions and the smaller broker lists, screen every new listing against a written buy box, run full diligence on anything that survives, build the valuation, draft the offer and the asset purchase terms, and then do one hundred percent of the operating work afterwards: the maintenance backlog, the support inbox, the content, the integrations, the SEO, the roadmap.

You provide the capital, sign, and move money through escrow. That is a genuinely small human footprint for the largest speed gain available.

Discipline

The diligence that stops this becoming an expensive lesson.

Most listings are junk. Expect to screen well over a hundred to buy one, and expect sellers to present their best fortnight as their run rate. Every one of these is a gate, not a preference.

  1. Revenue, from the processor, not a screenshotRead-only Stripe access covering 24 months. If they will not grant it, walk. There is no version of this where a screenshot is sufficient evidence, and you of all people know what a screenshot is worth.
  2. Monthly churn, logo and revenue separatelyAbove 8 to 10% monthly on a small subscription product means you are buying a leaking bucket and paying to refill it. Every point of churn reduction is worth 15 to 25% on the asset's value, which cuts both ways.
  3. Traffic concentrationWhere does every visitor come from? If ninety percent arrives from one channel, that channel is the actual asset and you are exposed to a single algorithm change. Price it accordingly or pass.
  4. Customer concentrationAny single customer above 20% of revenue means this is a consulting contract wearing a SaaS costume, and it will leave with the founder.
  5. Platform dependenceBuilt entirely on one company's API? One policy change ends it. This is not disqualifying, it is a discount.
  6. Support burden, measured in tickets per customer per monthThe one metric where we want a bad number. High support load is why the seller is exhausted and why the price is low, and it is the thing a fleet absorbs for nothing. Buy the tired founder's problem.
  7. TransferabilityCan it actually move? Undocumented cron jobs, a personal API key, a domain on someone else's registrar, a single point of failure in the founder's head. Get a full inventory before money moves.
  8. Legal cleanlinessTrademark position, no copyleft contamination in the dependency tree, no scraped data of questionable provenance, no accumulated personal information liability. Escrow always. Fraud is real at this end of the market.
  9. Why are they selling?The good answer is boredom, or a new job, or the maintenance backlog outgrew their weekends. The bad answer is a platform change they can see coming and you cannot. Ask directly, then verify independently.

The path with no capital

The three customers you already have.

If capital is not on the table, the second fastest path uses the one asset I have been ignoring: you already do HubSpot consulting, and HubSpot's gate is three active installs in unaffiliated production accounts.

Almost no marketplace developer can clear that gate quickly. You can, because you have existing client relationships and a decade of credibility with exactly the people who run the portals in question. That is not cold outreach and it does not violate your constraint. It is asking three people who already pay you for advice whether they want a tool that fixes a problem you have personally watched cost them money.

Do that, and HubSpot goes from a fourteen week gate to a two week one, and it is the highest revenue per customer surface in these documents.

Meanwhile, publish where there is no gate at all

npm, PyPI, the MCP directories and VS Code take minutes and ask nothing. Chrome takes a review of days. None of them will bill for you, so the pattern is the same in each: the listing is free and public, the premium path is a licence key checked against your own endpoint, and Stripe does the collecting.

These are cheap enough to run as a portfolio rather than a bet. Five of them is a week of fleet time, and the only clock left running is discovery.

The plan

Ninety days, three things at once.

These do not compete for my attention the way they would compete for yours, which is the point of the arrangement.

Days 1 to 14

  1. Write the buy box. Price ceiling, minimum monthly profit, acceptable churn, acceptable concentration, categories we understand. Written down first, so we are not negotiating with ourselves at 11pm over a listing that looks exciting.
  2. Start sourcing. Continuous screening of every new listing across the marketplaces against that buy box, with a one page verdict on anything that survives the first cut.
  3. Publish two zero-gate products into VS Code and the MCP directories, with licence-key billing wired to Stripe from day one so we learn the conversion mechanics on something small.
  4. Start the snapshot store. Half a day of work, a few dollars a month, and it begins accumulating the one asset that cannot be bought later. Whatever else happens, this should be recording by Friday.

Days 15 to 45

  1. Full diligence on three to five shortlisted assets, to the nine gates above, with a written valuation and a walk-away price for each.
  2. Three client conversations about the first HubSpot tool. Yours to have, and the only part of this plan that needs your voice.
  3. Three more zero-gate products, chosen from whatever the first two taught us about which listings actually get found.

Days 46 to 90

  1. Close one acquisition through escrow, and run the neglected-maintenance program against it immediately: the support backlog, the broken integration, the dead content, the churn causes.
  2. Submit the HubSpot listing once the three installs are live and active.
  3. Report actual numbers against these estimates, and revise the plan against what the market said rather than what I predicted.

Honesty

What this gets you, and what it does not.

A portfolio of acquired micro-assets and zero-gate utilities, run by a fleet, realistically reaches five to fifteen thousand a month within twelve to eighteen months if it works. That is a good cashflow business. It is not a large company, and it never becomes one, because inherited distribution comes with an inherited ceiling.

That is the price of speed, and it is a fair one. It is also why the snapshot store goes up in week one regardless: it costs almost nothing, it is the only thing here with a ceiling worth having, and every day we do not run it is a day of history that can never be recovered.

Kill criteria, written now

  • Acquisition: if a hundred and fifty screened listings produce nothing that clears all nine gates, the buy box is wrong or the market is picked over. Stop and reprice rather than relaxing the gates.
  • Acquisition, post-close: if revenue is below the pre-purchase run rate after ninety days of fleet work, the decline was structural and we misread it. Sell, take the lesson, do not average down.
  • Zero-gate products: if five listings produce under fifty installs between them in sixty days, discovery on those surfaces does not work for our category.
  • HubSpot: if three existing clients will not install a free tool that fixes a problem they have paid you to diagnose, the product thesis is wrong, and no amount of marketplace ranking would have saved it.

What only you can do

The capital and the escrow. Stripe and its identity checks. A verified organisation and domain if we go the GitHub route. The three client conversations. And being the named buyer and operator of record, which is not paperwork: it is what makes the whole arrangement legitimate.

The decision

The fork is capital.

With money to deploy, acquisition is the fastest legitimate path to profitable that exists, and I can start screening today. Without it, the answer is your three HubSpot clients and a shelf of zero-gate listings, which is slower but costs nothing except tokens.
