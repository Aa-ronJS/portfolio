Guides · Privacy impact assessment

Privacy impact assessments without the consultancy.

A PIA is an afternoon of structured questions, not a $30,000 engagement. When a small business needs one, and the eight steps that cover it.

A privacy impact assessment answers one question before a change instead of after it: if we do this, what could go wrong for the people whose information is involved — and what will we do about it? Government agencies must run PIAs for high-privacy-risk projects; for businesses the OAIC recommends them as practice. Consultancies have built a genre of them that runs to eighty pages. For a 2–50 person business, the useful version fits on four.

When one is actually warranted

  • A new tool that touches people's data — a CRM, a marketing platform, an AI assistant, a screening service.
  • A new kind of collection — you start asking for ID documents, health details, biometrics, or anything you didn't collect before.
  • A new flow — sharing with a referral partner, a franchisor, an offshore processor.
  • Automation of a decision about people — scoring, screening, triage. This is the sharpest case: from 10 December 2026, covered entities must disclose such automated decision-making in their privacy policies, and you cannot disclose accurately what you haven't examined.

The eight steps

1 — Describe the change in five sentences

What's the tool or process, who runs it, what decisions or actions come out of it. If you can't describe it plainly, that's the first finding.

2 — List the information involved

Exactly which fields: names, contact details, dates of birth, ID documents, financial or health details. Mark anything the Privacy Act treats as sensitive — it carries consent requirements and heavier weight in every later judgement.

3 — Map the flow

Where the data enters, where it's stored, who can see it, where it's sent — including the vendor's storage location. This is the APP 8 question: overseas hosting keeps you accountable for what happens there.

4 — Check it against the promises you've made

Your privacy policy and collection notices say what you use information for. Does the new thing fit those purposes, or does it need consent — or a policy update first?

5 — Ask what could go wrong, concretely

Not "risk of data breach" in the abstract: the screening tool keeps copies of applicant IDs indefinitely; the AI notetaker sends audio offshore; the shared inbox exposes client files to casual staff. Three to six specific failure modes, written down.

6 — Decide the controls

For each failure mode: avoid it (don't collect the field), reduce it (retention setting, access limits, contract clause), or accept it knowingly. "We looked and accepted it" is a legitimate outcome; "we never looked" is the indefensible one.

7 — Update the artefacts

The register gains a row, the policy gains a line if purposes changed, the ADM disclosure gains the new tool if it decides things about people. This is where the PIA stops being a document and becomes your actual state.

8 — Date it, file it, calendar the review

Signed, dated, kept with the register, reviewed when the tool changes or annually. A filed PIA is evidence of taking privacy seriously that exists before anything goes wrong — the cheapest good-faith evidence there is.
