The problem nobody prices in

Your business is worth less than you think.

Not on revenue. On transferability. When a buyer looks at a business where the quoting, the key relationships, the pricing rules and the "how we actually do it" all live in the owner's head, they are not buying a business — they are buying a job that loses its only qualified employee at settlement. They discount for that, or they walk.

  • Owner dependence is the number one deal-killer. "Owner too involved in daily operations" tops the reasons deals collapse in broker surveys, ahead of price.
  • The discount is 20 to 30 per cent. That's the gap between an owner-dependent business and the same business with systems a buyer can take over. On a million-dollar sale, that discount is a house.
  • Everyone starts too late. Most owners begin preparing about 18 months before they need to sell — after the burnout, the health scare, or the offer that fell through. The fixes need longer than that to show up in the books.
  • The advice industry stops at advice. Brokers and exit planners will tell you to "document your processes and reduce key-person risk". True. None of them sit with you and actually do it.

Why this book exists

Everyone tells you to document. Nobody does it with you.

There is a hole in the market between two industries. On one side, exit advisers and brokers, who will diagnose owner dependence and hand you a checklist. On the other, technical writers, who will sell you a binder of procedures nobody in your business will ever open. The actual work — getting the rules out of your head and into systems your team runs without you — belongs to neither of them.

It used to be genuinely expensive work: weeks of a consultant shadowing you, at rates only corporates pay. That is the part that has changed. The book is that craft, turned into a sequence an owner can run in the evenings. Not a valuation guide, not legal advice, no "mindset". The work.

Part 1

The audit: find out how trapped you are.

The holiday test — what actually breaks if you disappear for six weeks — run honestly, on paper, in one afternoon. Which decisions only you can make, which relationships only you hold, which rules only you know. Most owners have never measured this, and the number is worse than they think. It is also the exact list a buyer's accountant will build about you, so you may as well build it first.

Part 2

The extraction: get it out of your head at talking speed.

You do not write an operations manual. You say one — into a recorder, walking through each job as you actually do it, exceptions and war stories included — and AI does the typing, structuring and formatting. The chapter is the interview script: the questions that surface the rules you don't know you're applying, and the checking pass that catches what the transcript got wrong. An evening per process, not a quarter.

Part 3

The handoff: turn documents into a business.

A manual nobody opens changes nothing. This part moves each documented process to its new home: a person with real authority to run it, an automation where no judgement is needed, a checklist where it is. Includes the delegation ladder for owners who have tried delegating before and taken it all back, and the fortnight test — the deliberate absence that proves which handoffs held.

Before you buy

This is not for everyone.

  • Not for corporates. If you have an operations team and a change process, you already pay people for this. It's written for owner-run businesses, roughly two to fifty people.
  • Not a valuation, legal or tax guide. Nothing in it prices your business or structures your sale. That is licensed work — the book tells you when to buy it and what to have ready so it costs less.
  • Not for owners who want to stay the hero. If secretly you like being the only one who can do it, the book will annoy you. The whole method is about making you unnecessary.
  • Not magic AI. The AI types, structures and drafts. It cannot know your rules. If someone tells you an agent can systemize your business unsupervised, keep your hand on your wallet.

  • "I'm not selling for years."

    That's the right time — the discount is priced on the last few years of how the business ran, and most owners start 18 months out, which is too late. And every part pays now: real holidays, easier hiring, fewer 7am phone calls, whether or not you ever sell.

  • "Can't I just ask ChatGPT to write my SOPs?"

    It will write beautiful procedures for a business that doesn't exist. The value isn't the typing — it's the questions that extract your actual rules, and the checking that catches what the transcript got wrong. That method is the book. The typing was never the hard part.

  • "Why is it $9 and not free?"

    Free PDFs get skimmed and shelved. A small price means it gets read, and it means I owe you a refund if it isn't worth it — which keeps me honest in a way a free download doesn't.

  • "Is this a funnel into consulting?"

    The last chapter says plainly what working with me looks like, because some owners will want the extraction run for them. But the book is written so you can do it yourself — that's the test it has to pass to be worth your nine dollars.

Questions

Asked and answered.

Does it apply to my industry?

If the business depends on things only you know or only you can do, yes. The examples run across trades, agencies, clinics, wholesale and manufacturing, because the pattern is the same everywhere: the owner is the operating system.

Do I need to be technical?

No. Part 1 is pen and paper. Part 2 needs a phone that records and an AI assistant — any of the mainstream ones work, and the book shows the exact prompts and the checking routine. Part 3 is management, not technology.

The audit is an afternoon. Extraction runs at roughly an evening per process, and most businesses have ten to twenty processes that matter. The handoff and the fortnight test play out over a few months of normal trading. It's designed to run alongside the day job, because you don't have another option.
