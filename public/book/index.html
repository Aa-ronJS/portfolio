Where this comes from

I'm usually the second person on the project.

I've been a business analyst since 2015 — mining, policing, federal service delivery, local government — and building the automation myself since 2016. Which means for ten years my job has mostly been arriving after somebody's automation project has stalled, and working out what the first person meant.

The pattern is always the same. Nobody wrote down the rules the business really runs on, so the thing that got built runs on guesses. One charity's donor records had been merged on email address instead of their account number, and their donation totals were out by $3.8 million. Nobody had worked out why. Finding it took analysis; fixing it took a builder; and the projects that go wrong are the ones where those are two different people with a handover in between.

The book is the method I use to be both people at once, written so a business owner can run it — with their own hands, with one contractor, or with an AI coding agent doing the typing. It is the brief I would hand a delivery team, taught.

The book

Done, not documented.

Four parts, in the order the work actually happens. Not theory — each part ends with the artefact you should now be holding: a one-page spec, a working automation, a test that could actually fail, and a system your own people can change.

Part 1

The spec: one page, before anything is built.

What the process must do, what it must never do, the exceptions, and what finished looks like — pulled out of the people who actually run it, including the rules that only exist in somebody's head. This page is most of the value, it costs nothing but an afternoon, and it is the part every failed project skipped.

Part 2

The build: small, boring, and end to end.

Why the right first automation is the whole of one small process rather than a slice of a big one. Choosing between Zapier, Make and n8n on what actually differs. Where an AI coding agent genuinely replaces a contractor, and the specific ways it will build the wrong thing beautifully if the spec from Part 1 doesn't exist.

Part 3

The proof: "done" is a claim, not a fact.

How to check the result against something the builder doesn't control — the source system, the accounts, the company's own website — so you never again run for months on a report that is structurally incapable of failing. Part 4

The handover that isn't one.

How to keep the rules written down as the system changes, so you are never hostage to the person who built it — including when that person was me, and including when it was an AI. The maintenance habit that takes twenty minutes a month and is the difference between an asset and a time bomb.

Before you buy

This is not for everyone.

  • Not for enterprises. If you have a change advisory board, you already have people for this. The book is written for businesses of roughly two to fifty.
  • Not a get-rich book. Nothing in it promises revenue. It removes retyping, reconciliation errors and dependence on whoever built the last thing.
  • Not a tool tutorial. Screenshots of Zapier's interface age in a month. The method is the point; the tools are examples.
  • Not magic AI. If you want to hear that an agent will run your business unsupervised, that book is someone else's, and it is wrong.

  • "I don't have time to read a book about this."

    It's short on purpose — an evening, cover to cover. The alternative is the time you already lose: the retyping is happening today, on payroll, whether or not you ever measure it.

  • "Why is it $9 and not free?"

    Free PDFs get skimmed and shelved. A small price means it's read, and it means I owe you a refund if it's not worth it — which keeps me honest in a way a free download doesn't.

  • "Is this just a funnel into consulting?"

    The last chapter does say what working with me looks like, because some readers will want a builder. But the book is written to make you not need one for your first project — that's the test it has to pass to be worth your nine dollars.

  • "Who is Aaron to tell me this?"

    Fair question, and the answer is checkable: the main site names the systems, shows two live tools you can use right now, and the code behind them is public. No screenshots of dashboards, no rented Lamborghini.

Questions

Asked and answered.

Do I need to be technical?

No. Part 1 — the part that carries most of the value — is pen and paper. Parts 2 and 3 assume you can follow instructions in a web app or sit beside someone who can, and the book tells you honestly when a step is worth handing to a professional instead.

Which tools does it cover?

Zapier, Make and n8n for the plumbing; HubSpot, Salesforce and Zoho where a CRM is involved; Claude Code where an AI agent is doing the building. The method doesn't depend on any of them, and the book says which chapters to skip if your stack differs.

My business is trades / retail / professional services. Does it apply?

The examples are service businesses — bookings, quotes, invoicing, job data — because that's where the retyping lives. If your week involves moving the same information between systems by hand, it applies.
