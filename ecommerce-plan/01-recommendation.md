# 01 — Recommendation

## What to sell

**Workplace safety consumables on automatic replenishment, sold to small
businesses, with a compliance record attached.**

Concretely, three product lines and one piece of software:

1. **Site first-aid kits and vehicle first-aid kits**, compliant with the Safe
   Work Australia model Code of Practice (Appendix E contents), sold upfront,
   bought wholesale from an Australian ARTG-listed sponsor such as Aero
   Healthcare. Every kit carries a QR label.
2. **A replenishment plan per kit** (billed annually by default): two
   scheduled refill packs a year, after-use refills triggered by a 20-second
   QR scan, expiry tracking, an audit log, and an annual compliance
   certificate the customer can hand to an inspector, insurer or head
   contractor.
3. **Expiry-managed add-ons**: AED pads and batteries shipped before they
   expire (pads A$50 to 120 a set, batteries A$150 to 300, every 2 to 5 years),
   eyewash, burns modules, and a PPE/sunscreen consumables pack for outdoor
   trades.
4. **The software**: a kit register, expiry engine, QR after-use reporting,
   automatic reorder, audit trail and certificate generator. Built by you.
   This is the product the incumbents do not have.

Ideal first customers: trades with utes (plumbers, electricians, builders,
landscapers), allied-health clinics, childcare centres, cafes and small
manufacturers in Adelaide. South Australia has roughly 160,000 small
businesses. Australia has 996,000 employing businesses, every one of which
must provide first aid and keep the kit stocked. Tradies must also keep a kit
in each work vehicle.

## Why this and not a consumer product

The five research streams converged on the same structural facts:

- **Paid acquisition no longer works for a new small consumer brand.**
  Median Meta ROAS across 35,000 brands was 1.93x in 2025. Break-even ROAS at
  a 30 percent contribution margin is 3.3x. A first order loses about A$29 on
  average; only the repeat order earns. Blended CAC is up 40 to 60 percent
  since 2023. A minimum meaningful Meta test costs A$60 to 100 a day for four
  to six weeks; anything less produces no signal.
- **The founders who made it did so with near-zero CAC.** Bro Glo (organic
  TikTok, US$974 start), Bare Performance Nutrition (five years of YouTube
  before ads), Bluebird Provisions (SEO and email, zero ads), Worm Bucket
  (Reddit and Google Shopping), Bell & Bone in Australia (in-store sampling,
  email, wholesale; one employee at A$180k a month). None won on paid social.
- **Cash locked in inventory kills more small brands than anything else.**
  A A$30k consumer launch has A$24k committed before the first sale and hits a
  A$17k trough on the second purchase order. Stax, Tuchuzy, Mosaic and Kodgem
  all died or stalled on inventory and fixed costs ahead of demand.
- **Consumer e-commerce is worth the least at exit.** Flippa's 2025 average
  is 1.4x profit, the lowest of any category. B2B subscription businesses
  with low churn trade at 4 to 7x EBITDA.
- **Your unfair advantage is not product design.** It is CRM, automation,
  data, a decade of WHS-adjacent work in mining, policing and government, and
  the ability to build the tool yourself. The research streams independently
  rated "niche B2B consumables" and "first-aid kit plus automated restock" as
  the best skill fit of every option examined.

The B2B replenishment model also has the best cash profile of anything
examined. Kits are sold upfront at a margin that covers acquisition, annual
plans are paid in advance, the wholesaler ships daily so you hold almost no
stock, there is no China import, no MOQ, no tariff exposure, and time to first
sale is weeks rather than the 14 to 18 weeks of a private-label import.

## The honest picture of the competition

This is not an empty market, and the first research pass understated that.
On direct verification:

- **Alsco First Aid** rents kits with quarterly technician visits on a fixed
  annual fee (price not published). Their model is people in vans; it is
  priced for larger sites.
- **The First Aid Store** sells a comprehensive kit subscription at A$49 with
  a six-monthly kit exchange, no lock-in.
- **First Aid Distributions** (East Bendigo) bundles "KitCheck", a web tool
  where the customer logs their own checks and expiry dates. It does not
  auto-ship, has no QR reporting, and depends on someone opening the kit and
  typing.
- **A1 First Aid, National First Aid, Cleanpro, St John** offer visit-based or
  order-it-yourself restocking.
- **First Aid Kits Australia** has a "restocking feature" on its web store
  (order refills in a few clicks).

What none of them do: sell the *compliance outcome* (a live register, an
after-use trigger, an expiry engine across kits and AEDs, a certificate) with
the goods shipping automatically and no visit. That gap is exactly a software
problem. It is also why the plan must be validated with real customers first:
the demand for kits is proven, the demand for *this way of buying them* is a
hypothesis until 20 businesses pay for it.

## Why not the other shortlisted products

| Candidate | Why it lost |
|---|---|
| Custom-fit windscreen sunshade for AU utes | Excellent unit economics (A$12 to 15 landed, A$69 to 99 retail) but on direct check Snap Shades, Sharkskin, Yowie Drive, Dingo Trails, WeatherTech and Amazon AU sellers all now sell custom-fit windscreen versions. One-off purchase, 15 to 20 percent repeat. Not defensible for a non-manufacturer. |
| Dog health consumables on subscription | The strongest consumer option and the documented fallback (see below). Loses to the B2B plan on capital (Australian contract-manufacturer MOQs), time to market (18 weeks plus), claims risk near the TGA line, and Petbarn/Amazon AU pressure. |
| 3D-printing consumables for Australia | Real demand growth and a freight moat on 1 kg spools, but price competition from Bambu selling direct, and inventory across dozens of colours and materials is exactly the cash trap the evidence warns about. |
| Aging-in-place products | Fast-growing buyer cohort, underserved, but a curation business with no repeat engine until you private-label, and some items are TGA medical devices. Good second business. |
| Draught-proofing kit with a measurement configurator | Clever, 70 to 80 percent margin, but a single strong Australian-made incumbent (EcoMaster), seasonal, and one-off. |
| Supplements, sunscreen, skincare | Highest margins, highest regulatory and advertising friction, most expensive CPCs. TGA is tightening sunscreen after CHOICE found 16 of 20 failed SPF claims. Not a first business. |
| Dropshipping, print-on-demand, curated Faire retail, subscription boxes | Structurally dead for a US-facing seller after de minimis ended; 2 to 5 percent net for curated retail; 60 to 70 percent of box subscribers gone by order three. |

## Validation gates (the plan stops if these fail)

| Gate | By when | Pass condition | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 Supply | Week 2 | Written trade pricing from at least two ARTG-listed wholesalers giving 45 percent or better gross margin on kits at your retail price, and refill packs at or under A$12 | Reprice, or go to the fallback |
| G2 Demand | Week 8 | 20 paying accounts in Adelaide (kits bought plus plan started), of which at least 10 are on annual billing | Interview every "no", fix the offer once, extend to week 12. Two failures: fallback |
| G3 Retention | Month 6 | Monthly logo churn under 4 percent; at least 60 percent of accounts have scanned a QR or received an after-use refill | The software is not valued; sell kits and refills without the plan, or fallback |
| G4 Scale channel | Month 9 | At least one partner channel (WHS consultant, bookkeeper, industry association, franchise group) producing 10 or more accounts a month without your presence | Stay founder-led and cap ambition at a good solo income |

## Risks and how the plan handles them

- **Sales-limited growth.** This is a business you sell, not one that sells
  itself, at least for the first 200 accounts. The plan budgets your time
  honestly (no salary in year one), designs the partner channel from month
  four, and builds self-serve from day one so partner referrals convert
  without you.
- **Low ARPA.** A typical account is worth about A$35 a month recurring plus
  A$240 of kits upfront. Covering a A$7k monthly draw needs roughly 380
  accounts. The levers are multi-kit accounts (fleets, multi-site clinics,
  childcare groups), the PPE add-on, and AED consumables. Target average kits
  per account, not just account count.
- **Incumbent response.** First Aid Distributions could add auto-ship to
  KitCheck. Your defence is speed, the after-use QR loop (which creates
  usage data they do not have), and owning the customer relationship in one
  state before going national.
- **Regulatory.** Kits and bandages are ARTG-listed goods; you resell them
  and the sponsor carries the listing. Do not become a sponsor, do not
  repackage sterile items, and keep every claim to "compliant with the model
  Code of Practice", never "TGA approved". Mandatory injury reporting to the
  ACCC applies to all suppliers.
- **Customer concentration.** Do not chase one 200-kit customer in year one.
  A single account over 10 percent of revenue is a valuation discount and a
  churn risk.

## The fallback: dog health consumables on subscription

If G2 fails twice, the consumer option with the best evidence is a
premium Australian-made dog dental or gut-health chew sold on subscription
with email as the profit engine, on the Bell & Bone template. Australian
online pet is growing 15 to 20 percent a year, consumables repeat at four to
eight week cadence, gross margins are 50 to 65 percent, and the Australian
ingredient story supports the roughly 10 percent premium that "Australian
made" commands. It needs A$15 to 25k of inventory from an Australian contract
manufacturer, an 18 to 24 week runway, founder-led short-form video at four to
six posts a week for eight weeks before launch, in-store sampling with
independent pet retailers, and no paid social until 20 reviews and a working
email flow exist. It is a real business, and a harder one.
