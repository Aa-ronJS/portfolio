Bench 1 · Signal generator, spectrum analyser, oscilloscope

Signals and the spectrum

A radio signal is a steady wave, the carrier, that gets wiggled to carry voice or data. The wiggling is called modulation. Here you generate one and look at it three ways, the same way a radio tech does on a real bench.

1.1Find the noise floor

Every receiver hears a hiss of random thermal noise, roughly −174 dBm in every hertz of bandwidth, plus what the receiver adds itself. A signal below that floor is gone.

Look for: drag the level down from −90 to −150 dBm. The carrier spike sinks into the grass. That grass is the noise floor.

1.2Modulation takes up room

A bare carrier is a single line. Add voice and it spreads out. FM spreads more as you raise the deviation (how far the frequency swings).

Look for: raise deviation past 5 kHz and the signal spills outside the 12.5 kHz channel box into the neighbour's channel. That is why deviation is set in the radio's programming and not left to chance.

1.3Read a TETRA constellation

TETRA sends 2 bits at a time by jumping the phase of the carrier. The receiver view plots each jump as a dot. Eight clean clusters means clean data.

Look for: lower the level towards −120 dBm. The dots smear into each other and the symbol error counter climbs. That is a weak signal turning into broken audio.

1.4Neighbour vs same channel

A strong transmitter next door is mostly blocked by the receiver's filter. The same transmitter on your channel is not.

Look for: the eye diagram stays open with a loud neighbour. Switch the second transmitter to the same channel and the eye slams shut. This is why channels must be coordinated across a site.

Units you will meetdBm is power compared to one milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW, +30 dBm = 1 W, +40 dBm = 10 W. Every 3 dB doubles or halves the power; every 10 dB is ×10. A radio can still decode signals around −110 dBm, which is 0.00000000000001 mW.

Bench 2 · Transmitter, coax, wattmeter, antenna

Cable and antenna

Power leaves the radio, travels down coax cable, and has to get into the antenna. Cable eats power as heat. A bad antenna or a wet connector bounces power back down the cable. This bench shows where every watt goes.

Lab sheet

2.1Thin cable cooks your power

Cable loss is quoted in dB per 100 m and gets worse as frequency goes up.

Look for: 60 m of RG-58 at 420 MHz throws away most of a 25 W radio. Swap to ⅞″ heliax and most of it arrives. Underground base stations use the fat stuff for this reason.

2.2A crushed cable

Loaders and rock falls damage cable. A short or open sends all the power back.

Look for: the standing wave: fixed humps and dead spots instead of a smooth travelling wave. The reflected needle jumps and the radio protects itself by cutting power.

2.3Long cable hides a fault

Reflected power has to travel back down the lossy cable before the meter sees it.

Look for: VSWR at the antenna is terrible, but VSWR at the radio looks almost fine. Always measure as close to the antenna as you can, or use a cable analyser that measures distance to fault.

2.4Dummy load fools the meter

Look for: perfect VSWR, full forward power, and zero radiated. A perfect match only means the power went somewhere. It does not mean it went into the air.

VSWR in one line1.0 is perfect. Under 1.5 is good. Above 2.0 someone should look at it. Above 3.0 most radios turn their own power down to protect themselves.

Bench 3 · Coverage planner and drive test

Radio underground

Rock swallows UHF radio. Signal only gets around a mine by travelling along the tunnels, and it loses a lot at every corner. So underground systems either run a leaky feeder cable (a cable with slots that leaks signal along its length) down every drive, or put base station antennas at junctions, or both. Build a system on this example mine level and see what works.

3.1Corners kill UHF

One TETRA site in the middle of the main drive.

Look for: strong signal straight down the drive for hundreds of metres, then each crosscut goes amber and red within a few corners. Move the site with the TETRA tool (click to remove, click to place).

3.2Leaky feeder needs amplifiers

A leaky feeder loses a few dB every 100 m. Without amplifiers the far end goes dead.

Look for: the deep end in red. Press Auto-place amplifiers and it lights up. Then switch to Be heard: every amplifier also adds noise on the way back, so the base hears a little less with each one.

3.3Hear but not be heard

Look for: flip between Hear the base and Be heard. The 1 W TETRA handheld on a belt gives out long before the 10 W base does. That gap is where people say "base, base, do you read?" and nobody answers.

3.4Change the band

Keep the hybrid design and switch between VHF, UHF and 800 MHz.

Look for: VHF turns corners better but dies fast in straight drives because the wave is too big for the tunnel. 800 MHz flies down straight drives and dies at every corner.

Why fade marginSignal flickers up and down by 10 to 20 dB as you walk, because reflections off walls and vehicles add and cancel. Designers add a fade margin on top of the radio's sensitivity so it still works at the bottom of a flicker.

Bench 4 · Timeslots, bit errors, and what it sounds like

Digital voice

DMR and TETRA both chop your voice into small digital packets and squeeze several conversations onto one frequency by taking turns very fast. This is called TDMA. When the signal gets weak, analog radio gets hissy. Digital stays perfect, then falls off a cliff.

Both work out to 6.25 kHz per voice path. The difference is not efficiency. It is what the network does with the slots: TETRA hands them out on demand from a central switch; basic DMR repeaters give each slot a fixed job.

Lab sheet

4.1The cliff

Look for: play DMR at −108 dBm: clean. Slide down slowly to −122. It stays clean, then breaks into chirps and gaps over just a few dB. Do the same with analog FM: the hiss creeps in gradually and you can still make out words.

4.2Walking makes it worse

Look for: tick Walking. The average signal is the same but the curves slide right by roughly 8 to 10 dB. That gap is the fade margin from bench 3, measured.

4.3Fill the slots

Turn every conversation on. A TETRA carrier has 3 voice slots. Slot 1 is reserved for the control channel that every radio listens to for instructions.

Look for: the sync and training bits in the middle of each burst. The receiver uses them to lock on to each burst, which is how it copes with reflections underground.

Why digital sounds "robotic"The voice is compressed by a codec (AMBE+2 in DMR, ACELP in TETRA) down to a few thousand bits per second. When a packet is lost, the codec repeats the last sound to cover the gap. Lose several in a row and you hear warbling, then silence.

Bench 6 · From the lab to the mine

Your project

Moving the underground radio from the existing Simoco system to a DAMM platform running TETRA. What the parts are, what changes, and the questions to put to engineers and vendors so you can run the job without being an RF engineer yourself.

The picture in one paragraph

Today the underground runs on Simoco radio equipment. Simoco sells DMR, analog and older MPT1327 trunked systems, and underground those are usually carried along the drives by a leaky feeder with amplifiers every few hundred metres. The new system is built on DAMM (a Danish maker whose TetraFlex base stations can run TETRA and DMR on the same hardware). So the end state is likely TETRA for the main operational network (trunked talkgroups, control room console, data, emergency features), with DMR where it still makes sense, all on DAMM infrastructure. During the changeover, the old Simoco system and the new DAMM system have to run side by side and talk to each other, so an emergency on one is heard on the other. The hard parts are coverage underground, the cutover while the mine keeps running, and the link between old and new.

One DAMM detail that changes the pictureA classic TETRA network has one big central switch. DAMM TetraFlex spreads the switching across the base stations themselves, linked over an IP network. That removes the single box whose failure takes everything down, but it makes the IP network (fibre, switches, its power) part of the radio system. On bench 5, read "TETRA switch" as "call control", which in a DAMM system lives in the base stations.

Assumptions in this lab: the existing Simoco system is treated as DMR or analog channels on a leaky feeder, which is the most common underground arrangement. Check which Simoco system and which technology (DMR Tier II, Tier III, analog, MPT1327) the site actually has. The mine level, power levels and frequencies are realistic examples, not Olympic Dam's actual design. Licensed frequencies, site layouts and equipment choices come from the site's RF design and the ACMA licence.
