How it works

Real sources, one score, nothing invented.

Tools like this usually show you a big audience number and ask for your email before they tell you where it came from. This one asks public systems that anybody can query, shows the query, and scores what came back out of 100 on four things a paying market has to have.

01

Audience. Are people there at all?

How many people read the topic's Wikipedia article each month, how many podcasts are made about it, and how many books have been written on it. Wikipedia pageviews are the most honest public number there is: nobody buys them and nobody bots them for a hobby.

02

Demand. Are they looking for help?

Google only autocompletes a phrase when enough people have typed it. So the tool asks nineteen variations, from "how to" to "near me" to "membership", and counts what Google is willing to finish. A phrase Google completes is a phrase people search.

03

Proof of pay. Does money already change hands?

Paid ebooks, audiobooks and apps on the topic in Apple's stores, with their real prices and rating counts, plus how many buying-intent phrases Google completes: course, class, kit, subscription, cost. If nobody sells anything, nobody is buying.

04

Momentum. Growing, flat or fading?

The last six months of Wikipedia interest against the six before, and the share of the topic's podcasts that have published an episode in the last year. A big audience that is walking away is a worse bet than a small one arriving.

Each part is worth 25. A source that fails to answer is left out and the rest are rescaled, and the report says which. Facebook groups, Discord servers and Skool communities cannot be counted from the outside, so instead of a guess the report gives you the search links to go and look.
