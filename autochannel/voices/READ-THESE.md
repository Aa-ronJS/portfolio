# Seven takes to record

One recording per passage, 20–30 seconds each. The clone copies how you
sound in the take (pace, mood, closeness), so *perform* each one the way
that channel should sound. Read each passage three times and keep the
best take.

**Setup:** quiet room with soft stuff around you (a wardrobe full of
clothes is perfect), no fan or fridge hum, phone 20 cm from your mouth,
voice memo app. Leave a second of silence at the start and end. Save each
take named as below (`aaron-dry.m4a` etc.).

---

### 1. `aaron-dry`: Aaron Steele
*Like telling a mate something surprising at the pub. Relaxed, a bit amused.*

> You know those little gaps in bridges? The ones that go clunk under
> your tyres? I always assumed someone had just measured wrong. Turns out
> they're the most important part of the whole thing. On a hot day, a
> big bridge can grow longer by about the length of a car. Without those
> gaps, it would simply push itself apart. So that clunk is the bridge
> breathing. Which is either reassuring or not, depending on how you feel
> about bridges.

### 2. `aaron-low`: The Second Knock
*Low, close to the mic, slow. It's 2am and you'd rather not be telling this.*

> I worked nights at a servo outside town for about a year. Nothing ever
> happened. That was the point. Then one night, around three, a car
> pulled in and parked at the far pump. Engine running. Lights off.
> Nobody got out. I watched it for twenty minutes. When I finally walked
> over, the driver's seat was empty, and the radio was playing the song
> I'd been humming all night. Quietly. Like it didn't want to wake anyone.

### 3. `aaron-brisk`: Red String Files
*Quick and sharp, a detective laying out evidence. Enjoying it.*

> Here's the problem with the ending. Everyone says the hero wins. But
> look at the last shot again. The clock on the wall says ten past four.
> Now go back to the opening scene. Same room, same clock, ten past four.
> That's not a mistake. The designers reused nothing else in that room.
> So either it's the laziest coincidence in the whole game, or the story
> never moved at all, and we spent forty hours walking in a circle.

### 4. `aaron-measured`: Ledger of the Odd
*Steady documentary. Serious, respectful, fascinated.*

> In July of fifteen eighteen, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into a
> narrow street in Strasbourg and began to dance. She did not stop for
> days. Within a week, dozens had joined her. Within a month, the city
> council had a crisis on its hands, and a solution that made it worse.
> They built a stage. They hired musicians. They believed the only cure
> for dancing was more dancing. Historians are still arguing about why.

### 5. `aaron-hushed`: Treeline Reports
*Quiet campfire voice. Curious, open-minded, a little wary.*

> The report came in from a ranger in nineteen seventy-seven. He'd been
> walking the fire trail just after dusk when the bush went silent. Not
> quiet. Silent. No birds, no insects, nothing. Then, about thirty metres
> ahead, something stepped across the track. Upright. Taller than him.
> He said it didn't run. It looked at him for a moment, and walked into
> the trees like it had somewhere better to be. He never went back.

### 6. `aaron-sleep`: Lamplight Lectures
*The slowest, softest version of you. Reading to someone half asleep. Long pauses.*

> Get comfortable. Let your shoulders drop. Tonight we're going to spend
> some time with an old lighthouse keeper, on a small island, a long time
> ago. His day began in the late afternoon, when the light outside turned
> gold. He would climb the narrow stairs, slowly, one hand on the cool
> stone wall, and polish the great glass lens until it shone. Then he
> would light the lamp, and settle into his chair, and listen to the sea.

### 7. `aaron-warm`: Blind Spot Lab
*Warm, curious teacher. A little playful. You love how strange people are.*

> Quick question. Is the population of Turkey more or less than thirty
> five million? Got an answer? Now guess the actual number. Here's the
> funny part. If I'd asked about a hundred million instead, your guess
> would have been much higher, even though that first number told you
> absolutely nothing. Your brain grabbed it anyway, and held on. It's
> called anchoring, and it's quietly running half your decisions.
