What actually changed

Half the internet has the date wrong.

You may have read that "the small business exemption ends on 10 December 2026". It doesn't — that date belongs to a different obligation. Compliance advice that starts with a wrong fact is not advice. Here is what has actually happened, dated, with sources at the bottom of this page.

Since Dec 2022

The penalties stopped being trivial.

For serious or repeated interference with privacy, the maximum civil penalty is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover. Small operators won't see numbers like that — but the regulator's posture changed with them, and mid- and low-tier penalties now exist for ordinary failures.

Since Jun 2025

Individuals can now sue.

The new statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy means a person no longer needs the regulator to act — they can bring a claim themselves. A mishandled client file is no longer only a complaints-process problem.

1 Jul 2026

~100,000 businesses lost the exemption. Quietly.

The AML/CTF tranche-2 reforms made real estate agents, conveyancers, lawyers, accountants and dealers in precious metals reporting entities — and reporting entities do not get the small business exemption. If that's you, the 13 Australian Privacy Principles apply to you today, at any turnover.

10 Dec 2026

Privacy policies must disclose automated decisions.

From this date, covered entities using personal information in automated decision-making that could significantly affect someone — credit scoring, tenant screening tools, AI-assisted triage — must say so in their privacy policy: what information, what decisions. If you've bolted AI tools onto your workflow this year, this is yours.

Not yet law

Full removal of the $3M exemption: agreed, undated.

Government agreed in principle in 2023 to remove the small business exemption entirely — around 2.3–2.5 million businesses. The August 2026 exposure draft bill does not include it, and no commencement date exists. The honest read: the direction is one-way, the deadline is unknown, and preparing while it's cheap beats scrambling when a date lands.

Covered already?

The exemption has more holes than people think.

"Under $3M turnover" was never the whole test. The Act already applies to you — at any size — if any of these fit:

  • You became an AML/CTF reporting entity on 1 July 2026 — real estate, conveyancing, legal, accounting, trust and company services, precious metals dealing.
  • You provide a health service and hold health information — allied health, fitness and wellbeing included, not just clinics.
  • You trade in personal information — buying or selling lists, or disclosing customer data for a benefit.
  • You're a contractor to the Commonwealth under a contract that touches personal information.
  • Your turnover passed $3M — including one good year; coverage doesn't switch back off when revenue dips.
  • Your biggest clients require it anyway. Corporate and government supply chains increasingly demand Privacy Act-consistent handling from vendors regardless of the law's floor — the contract makes it binding for you either way.

Fair questions

Asked by every owner so far.

  • "We're still exempt. Why spend anything?"

    Three reasons that aren't the deadline: your bigger clients' contracts increasingly require it now; the new tort means an aggrieved person can sue now; and the exemption's removal is agreed in principle — doing it calmly this year is cheaper than doing it in a rush the year a date lands.

  • "Can't our IT provider handle it?"

    They handle part of it — APP 11 is security, and MFA and backups matter. But most of the 13 principles are about process and promises: what you collect, what you tell people, how you use it, how someone gets their file. That's operations, not hardware, which is why MSPs selling this as an IT project keep producing compliant servers inside non-compliant businesses.

  • "Can't I get AI to write a privacy policy?"

    It will write a beautiful one — for a business that doesn't exist. A policy that describes what you actually do requires knowing what you actually do, which is the data map. And the policy is one artefact out of thirteen obligations. Use AI for the typing; the kit gives it the questions.

  • "Why not just use a law firm?"

    Use one — at the end, where they're irreplaceable. A firm reviewing a finished data map and drafted pack bills a fraction of a firm interviewing your staff from scratch at partner rates. I do the operational build; your lawyer does the law. If you don't have one, I'll point you at firms that do fixed-fee review.

Questions

Asked and answered.

Does the Privacy Act actually apply to my business?

If your turnover is under $3M and none of the capture categories fit — you're not an AML/CTF reporting entity, don't provide health services, don't trade in personal information, aren't a Commonwealth contractor — then mostly not yet. The free readiness check walks the categories. If you're captured, all 13 APPs apply regardless of size.

What's the 10 December 2026 obligation everyone talks about?

From that date, covered entities whose privacy policies are silent on automated decision-making must fix that: if personal information feeds automated decisions that could significantly affect someone's rights or interests, the policy must say what information and what kinds of decisions. It is not the end of the small business exemption — that reform has no date yet.

What happens if the exemption removal becomes law?

Around 2.3–2.5 million businesses would need what the kit builds: a policy, a register, a breach plan, and practices that match. There will likely be a transition period — and a stampede. Kit buyers get updates for 12 months, so the version you buy now becomes the version that matches the bill.

We've already had a breach. Is it too late?

No, but the order changes: containment and assessment come first, and the notifiable data breaches scheme may put you on a 30-day clock. The breach plan in the kit covers it, and if it's live right now, skip the kit and get proper advice today — the OAIC's own guidance is genuinely good in an emergency.

Is this legal advice?

No. It's general information and operational tooling, built to be reviewed by a lawyer where your situation needs one — the templates mark those spots. Nothing here creates a lawyer-client relationship, and a firm's sign-off on your finished pack is money well spent.
