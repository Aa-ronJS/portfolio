What that actually proves

Three businesses, three price points, one public record. The mechanic works.

The useful reading is not "that idea is dead." It is that a charity, a small operator and a CoreLogic subsidiary all sustain themselves on data that any of us can legally download, and they coexist because they sell different things on top of the same rows: the charity sells civic participation, the operator sells filtered leads, the incumbent sells verified certainty. Nobody is really competing on the data. They are competing on what they do to it.

Then I checked whether the pattern repeats elsewhere. It does, and recently:

Trade licence registers

Two aggregators, both new

Eight states and territories, each with its own licence classes and terminology, no national register. WorkClear and TradieVerify have both appeared, normalising 1.6 million records behind a single REST API. Occupied.

Mining tenements

One state covered. Seven are not.

NextMaps runs a live map, due diligence and email alerts for new applications, transfers, surrenders and expiries, for Western Australia only. SA, VIC, TAS, QLD, NSW and NT all publish tenement data. No national aggregator exists.

The real finding

This is a land grab, and it is roughly eighteen months old. AI enabled solo operators are working through Australia's fragmented public registers one at a time, at $49 to $199 a month, undercutting incumbents charging $7,000 a year. The thesis is not speculative. It is being executed right now by people who are not you.

Which means three things: the mechanic is validated, target selection is the entire game, and being second into a register is close to worthless. Speed matters more than polish here, and picking correctly matters more than both.

The unfair advantage of doing this in Australia

Nobody owns a fact, and here they do not own the list either.

This play would be legally hazardous in Europe, which has a specific database right protecting the investment in compiling data even where no creativity exists. Australia deliberately went the other way, twice, at the highest level:

IceTV v Nine (2009, High Court) ended the "sweat of the brow" doctrine here. Effort spent compiling a database does not, by itself, earn copyright. Telstra v Phone Directories [2010] FCA 44, upheld on appeal, went further: copyright did not subsist in the White and Yellow Pages at all, because the directories were assembled by an automated system with no identifiable human author making an independent intellectual contribution.

Read that second holding again with this business in mind. The Australian courts have said that a machine generated compilation of facts has no copyright owner. That cuts towards us on the source data, and it also means our own compilation is not protected either, which is precisely why the moat has to be something other than the rows themselves. More on that below.

Where the actual risk is, and it is not copyright

Three separate questions, and copyright is the easy one. Contract: a site's terms of use can bind you regardless of copyright, so each source gets classified by its terms before we touch it, and anything that forbids automated access is either negotiated or dropped. The Privacy Act: many of these registers carry names and addresses of individuals, and republishing personal information at scale is a live obligation even when the source is public. Defamation and negligence: any product that implies a named company is in financial trouble is a legal exposure, not just a technical one.

None of that is fatal and all of it is manageable, but it is the one part of this plan where you should spend a few hundred dollars on an actual Australian solicitor rather than take my word for it. I am not one.

Selection

What makes a register worth owning.

This is the transferable part. Everything above is one worked example; the rubric below is the thing you keep, and it is what we score candidates against instead of guessing.

  1. FragmentationHow many independent sources must be reconciled to make the whole? One clean national API means no wall and no reason for you to exist. Eight state schemas is a real wall. Five hundred council websites is a fortress.
  2. Source volatilityHow often do the sources break? Counterintuitive, and important: breakage is an asset if you can afford the maintenance and a volunteer cannot. PlanningAlerts currently has around seventy of its scrapers marked possibly broken. That is not a criticism of them, it is the moat, measured.
  3. Unbackfillable historyDoes the source publish only its current state and overwrite the past? The single most important question on this list. See below.
  4. Event shapeDoes something discrete and dated happen that someone needs to know about within hours? A reference table does not sell subscriptions. A stream of dated events does, because alerts are the product and a table cannot be alerted on.
  5. A buyer with a deadlineWhose job or money depends on knowing first? Deadlines beat curiosity every time. Objection periods close, licences expire, tenements lapse, expenditure commitments fall due. Curiosity churns in month two.
  6. Demonstrated willingness to payIs somebody already charging real money? An expensive incumbent is good news, not bad. Cordell at $6,930 a year proves the value of the underlying record. An empty market usually means the record is worthless.
  7. Legal cleanlinessPublic record, permissive terms, minimal personal information, Australian jurisdiction.
  8. OccupancyHas an AI enabled solo already taken it in the last two years? Check this first, not last. I did it fourth and it cost us the original plan.

The moat

The only thing here a competitor cannot buy is the past.

Anyone can copy your schema, your pages and your pricing in a weekend, and after Telstra v Phone Directories they can arguably copy your rows too. So the moat cannot be the data. It has to be a time series that did not exist until you started recording it.

Most government registers publish current state and quietly overwrite. Who holds this tenement. What this licence's status is today. Which applications are currently on exhibition. Query it next year and last year is simply gone. Which means the moment you begin taking a dated, immutable snapshot every day, you start accumulating something no competitor can ever purchase, scrape or catch up on, and the gap widens every single day you keep running. A rival launching in eighteen months starts at zero history against your eighteen months, permanently.

And history is where the answerable questions live. Not "who holds this ground" but how often does this holder let ground lapse, which areas have churned three times in five years, whose licences are trending towards expiry, what is the median time from lodgement to decision at this council and is it getting worse. None of those can be answered from a snapshot of today, which is all the register itself offers, and all a new entrant will have.

Why the verification loop is not optional

The characteristic failure of this entire category is the silently dead feed. A council changes its portal, a scraper starts returning zero rows, and nothing anywhere reports an error, because zero rows is a perfectly valid answer. Months later somebody notices that a whole region stopped producing applications. PlanningAlerts has around seventy authorities in that state right now, and they are the good guys with ten years of practice.

This is your own method, pointed at data instead of code. An agent asserting the pipeline ran is a claim, not a fact. The fact is a second, independent process that re-fetches a random sample straight from the source, diffs it against what we stored, and shouts when they disagree or when a feed's volume falls off its own historical distribution.

Then publish it. A page that says we are currently failing to read four of sixty two sources, here they are, here is when each last succeeded is unusual enough to get linked, it is the exact claim the $7,000 incumbent will not make, and it is your brand in a single artefact.

Distribution

Four channels, none of which cost anything.

This was your original question and it deserves the detail. All four are pull, all four reward machine scale, and the fourth one barely existed two years ago.

Channel one

Programmatic pages that could not be written by hand

Thin templated pages get penalised now and deserve to. The test that separates the two: could a competent human write this page without our database? If yes, it is filler. A page per suburb, per council, per holder, per parcel, carrying counts, medians, trends and a dated history, passes that test because the data does not exist anywhere else in one place.

Ten thousand pages that each answer a real question is a moat. Ten thousand pages that each rephrase the same sentence is a penalty.

Channel two

Being the thing a language model cites

Underrated, and it is where the traffic is going. Models cite sources that are structured, dated, attributable and freely readable. Concretely that means clean semantic HTML, schema.org Dataset markup, stable permanent URLs, an explicit licence on the free tier, and an llms.txt.

The real requirement underneath the tactics: be the only place where the fact exists as one row. A model cites you when citing you is the shortest path to the answer.

Channel three

The MCP server as a shelf

You already ship these, which makes this nearly free. Listing in the MCP directories puts you in front of agent builders searching with intent, and every agent that wires you in becomes a recurring query rather than a one-off visit.

DA Leads shipped one. That is not a reason to avoid it, it is confirmation that the smartest operator in the adjacent market reached the same conclusion.

Channel four

The civic layer, which buys the best links in the country

Journalists, researchers, community groups and council staff link to free public interest data. Those are .gov.au, .edu.au and masthead domains, and they are the highest authority links available in Australia at any price.

They only arrive if the free tier is genuinely useful rather than a paywall tease. That is the trade: give away the lookup completely, and charge for being told without having to look.

The money

Priced under the incumbent by a factor of five.

Cordell's floor is $577.50 a month for one state and two users. That number is the whole opportunity: it is what the market has agreed the underlying record is worth, and it is far more than most of the people who need it can justify. Undercutting it by five to ten times is the only wedge a small operator ever gets, and it is a wide one.

  • FreeSearch, and one saved alert. This is not a funnel trick, it is the distribution channel. It has to be good enough that somebody links to it.
  • $39–79 / moUnlimited alerts, filters, saved searches, daily digest. The individual operator: the contractor, the agent, the consultant, the analyst. This tier is the volume.
  • $149–249 / moAPI, bulk export, and a direct push into their CRM. Your specific edge, and it is the same mechanic as Doorknock: the data does not arrive in a tab they forget to open, it arrives as records in HubSpot.
  • $500+ / moData licensing and history access. The tier that only exists because of the snapshot store, sold to people who want the time series rather than today.

The arithmetic you should hold me to

Two hundred customers at $79 is about $16,000 a month, and that is the realistic shape of a good outcome here, not a million dollar business. But free to paid conversion on a tool like this runs at one to three percent, so two hundred paying subscribers needs somewhere near ten thousand monthly free users, and getting there on organic search alone takes twelve to eighteen months, not the three to six I said in the first note.

I was too optimistic in that document and I would rather correct it now than have you discover it in month seven. This is a compounding asset, not a business that pays you this year.

Discipline

Written before we start, so they still count later.

Kill criteria are worthless if invented after the fact, so here they are now. If any of these hits, we stop and move the fleet to the next register on the list rather than defending a sunk year.

  • Month 3: no page ranking in the top twenty for any commercial query. The distribution thesis is wrong for this register, not merely early.
  • Month 6: fewer than 1,000 monthly free users. The funnel arithmetic cannot reach 200 paying from there, whatever we do next.
  • Month 6: source health cannot be held above 95% with agents doing the maintenance. If we cannot beat the volunteers, we do not have a moat, we have a hobby.
  • Any time: a well resourced incumbent releases the same thing free. Move, immediately, and take the pipeline with us.
  • Any time: the first hundred paying customers churn above 8% a month. The alerts are not tied to a deadline that actually matters.

Doing it

The first fortnight.

  1. The sweep. Score forty to sixty Australian public registers against the eight point rubric, including a genuine occupancy check on each. Output is a ranked table with evidence, not opinions. This is the step that would have saved the original plan.
  2. Pick, and get advice. One register. Then a short, specific brief to an Australian solicitor covering terms of use, personal information, and republication for the chosen dataset. A few hundred dollars against a year of work.
  3. Start recording before building anything. The snapshot store goes up first and starts taking dated copies on day one, because every day we delay is a day of history we can never have. It costs almost nothing to run and it is the entire moat. Nothing else needs to exist yet.
  4. Ingest, resolve, verify. One adapter per source, entity resolution across jurisdictions, and the independent verification loop running from the start rather than added after the first silent failure.
  5. Ship the free layer. Search, the programmatic pages, the public source health page, schema.org markup, and the MCP server. No payment wall yet, nothing to convert, just the thing people can link to.
  6. Then wait, visibly. Watch rankings and citations weekly. Billing goes in only once there is traffic to charge, because a paywall on an empty site teaches us nothing and costs us the links.

What only you can do

Domain and hosting under your name, Stripe and its identity checks, an ABN if there is not one already, the call on any source whose terms are ambiguous, and being the named human behind a public interest dataset. That last one is not administrative overhead, it is a functional requirement of channel four.

The decision

Say go and I will run the sweep.

The sweep is days of agent time, costs nothing but tokens, and ends with a ranked table of every Australian register worth owning, with the occupancy check already done. We should have started with it.
