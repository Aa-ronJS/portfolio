# The authority plan

Links, podcasts, partners, and every other way the site earns trust that
does not involve publishing another page. SEO-PLAN.md covers what lives on
the domain; this covers everything that points at it. Same author, same
rules: nothing here you would be embarrassed to explain on a call, and
everything sized for one person with a client load.

The strategic frame first, because it decides every tactic below. A new
domain with 82 good pages and zero links is a well-stocked shop on a street
nobody walks down. Google's systems (and now the AI answer engines) use
links and mentions as the tiebreak between pages of similar quality, and
your pages are competing against 20-year-old agencies with 20 years of
accumulated links. You will not out-link them, and you do not need to: the
targets in this plan are the long-tail and local queries where fifteen good
links and a live Google Business Profile beat their homepage authority,
plus the referral channels where a link is really a relationship that sends
work directly, with SEO as the side effect.

Time budget: **two focused hours a week, separate from the content hour**,
plus the occasional recorded conversation. Every section below says what
its hour buys. When in doubt, spend the hour on Tier 2 (people) over
Tier 4 (podcasts) over everything else.

A verification note that applies to every named directory, podcast and
publication in this plan: names were checked against what I knew when this
was written, and ecosystems drift. **Before pitching anything, spend two
minutes confirming it still exists, still publishes, and still fits.** A
pitch to a dead podcast is worse than no pitch.

## Tier 0: identity and citations (one afternoon, once)

Boring, foundational, and the only tier that is a checklist rather than a
practice. Search engines cross-reference the same business entity across
the web; inconsistency is quiet damage. Exact same name, description
story, and (once it exists) domain everywhere:

- **Google Business Profile.** Already drafted in OUTREACH.md; this is the
  single highest-value listing on the internet for you. Weekly posts keep
  it alive (SEO-PLAN.md owns that cadence).
- **Bing Places and Apple Business Connect.** Ten minutes each, near-zero
  ongoing effort, and Bing feeds several AI answer engines.
- **LinkedIn.** Profile rewritten to the site's story (the OUTREACH.md
  bios adapt directly), website field pointed at the domain, and the
  monthly repost habit from SEO-PLAN.md. LinkedIn is also where half of
  Tier 2 actually happens.
- **GitHub profile.** Bio and website link, pinned repos (portfolio,
  Linehaul), README profile page telling the same story. Your public code
  is a trust asset; make sure it points home.
- **Upwork and Expert360 profiles.** Marketplace links carry no SEO juice
  and full identity value: buyers cross-check you, and AI engines read
  the consistency.
- **The general Australian directories, selectively.** True Local,
  StartLocal, Yellow Pages online and similar: pick the handful that rank
  for "web developer adelaide" themselves (search it and see), fill those
  properly, ignore the rest. Fifty thin directory listings is a spam
  pattern; five real ones is a citation base.
- **Developer directories.** Clutch, GoodFirms and similar review-driven
  directories rank well for "app developers australia" queries. Free
  listings, and they become more valuable once the first two or three
  client reviews exist to display. List now, seed reviews as jobs finish.
- **Set up the listening posts** (they take minutes and serve every later
  tier): Google Alerts on your name and domain; F5Bot for Reddit and
  Hacker News mentions of your tools' names; Search Console's Links
  report bookmarked; Ahrefs Webmaster Tools (free tier) on the domain so
  you can see what links exist at all.

## Tier 1: ecosystem directories you have a claim to (the sharpest hour)

This is the tier most one-person shops never think of, and it converts the
twelve `/systems/` pages from content into positioning. Every platform you
wrote a guide about has some form of partner, consultant, advisor or
expert listing, and those listings are exactly where a buyer who already
owns that system goes looking for help. These links live on high-authority
domains, next to purchase intent, in tiny ponds:

- **Xero's ecosystem.** The advisor directory proper requires
  certification (a course and an exam; decide if the badge is worth the
  hours, it plausibly is given how many of the systems pages end in
  Xero). Short of that, the Xero community and app ecosystem discussions
  are places a person who demonstrably understands clearing accounts and
  payout reconciliation gets noticed by bookkeepers.
- **Field service platforms.** ServiceM8 runs a partner program with a
  public directory of consultants; simPRO and AroFlo have partner or
  implementer arrangements. Apply to each where the entry bar is
  reasonable; your systems page for that platform is the application's
  proof.
- **HubSpot.** The solutions provider tier is a paid program; below it,
  the community forum rewards genuinely useful answers with profile
  visibility. Answer the questions your HubSpot-to-Xero page already
  answers.
- **Practice management and RTO systems.** Cliniko, Halaxy, aXcelerate
  and VETtrak each have some mix of community forum, partner listing or
  recommended-consultant arrangement. Smaller ecosystems mean a helpful
  regular gets known platform-wide in months.
- **The method for every forum in this tier:** answer real questions with
  real answers, link your relevant guide only when it genuinely answers
  the thread, and let the profile link carry the SEO. Forum signature
  spam died in 2012; being the person whose answers get bookmarked did
  not.

## Tier 2: people who send work and links (the highest-payoff tier)

Every channel here is a referral relationship first and a link second.
Fifteen of these relationships outperform any quantity of directory
listings, and they compound.

- **Bookkeepers and accountants.** The single best referral source for
  every money-adjacent systems page: they see the ServiceM8 double entry
  and the unreconciled Shopify feeds first, they are trusted, and they do
  not want to fix it themselves. The move is small and personal: identify
  ten Adelaide bookkeeping practices (the Xero and MYOB communities are
  where they congregate), send the coffee email in the templates section,
  and hand them the systems guides as tools that make them look good with
  their own clients. A bookkeeper who links your WooCommerce-fees guide
  from their resources page has given you a link, an endorsement and a
  channel in one motion.
- **Marketing agencies without dev capacity.** Agencies sell websites and
  subcontract the builds. White-label or named, this is steady work and
  usually a partners-page link. The pitch is in the templates; the proof
  is the portfolio and the public build.
- **Fellow freelancers, adjacent and overflow.** Designers who need a
  builder, developers who hate WordPress, the booked-solid dev who needs
  somewhere to send the job they cannot take. Be the person whose name
  gets said. This is mostly showing up: the meetups in Tier 6, the
  communities in Tier 1, answered DMs.
- **Your own suppliers and tools.** Companies love publishing customer
  stories, and they link them. You use hosting, platforms, and half the
  systems you wrote about; offer a concrete testimonial with a real
  number in it to the ones you genuinely rate, on the explicit basis that
  it carries your name and site. Five minutes each, high-authority
  domains, entirely honest.
- **Past employers' networks, carefully.** Eleven years of colleagues now
  scattered across BHP, SA Police, Services Australia and the consulting
  world know exactly what your work is like. A LinkedIn post announcing
  the practice (once the domain is live) to that audience is worth more
  than any cold outreach in this plan. One post, no follow-up spam.

## Tier 3: launches (episodic, high-spike)

You have launchable assets most freelancers never will: Rain Check,
Doorknock, the pricing calculator, the Linehaul build. Each is one
launch-day effort with a long link tail:

- **Show HN on Hacker News** for the tools with a story ("Show HN: I got
  sick of Adelaide rain wrecking my week, so..."). Honest technical
  write-up, present in the comments all day, thick skin. One good Show HN
  outranks a year of directory submissions, and F5Bot tells you when the
  aftershock mentions happen.
- **Reddit, by the subreddit's rules.** r/webdev, r/smallbusiness,
  r/australianbusiness and trade-specific subs each have self-promotion
  rules; read them, and lead with the useful thing, not the link. A
  genuinely helpful comment history in a niche sub is also Tier 2 in
  disguise.
- **Tool directories and newsletters.** Free-tool roundups, dev
  newsletters, and "useful sites" listicles accept submissions and link
  forever. Batch-submit each tool once: an hour per tool, done.
- **Awesome lists and GitHub.** Linehaul and the tools belong in relevant
  curated lists (awesome-dotnet and friends) where they genuinely fit the
  list's bar. PRs to lists are public and reviewable; only submit where
  it honestly belongs.

## Tier 4: podcasts (the credibility multiplier)

Podcasts are the best authority format for you specifically, because your
advantages are conversational: the unusual career arc, the $3.8m story,
and a genuinely contrarian-but-honest take on AI. Every episode yields a
backlink from the show notes, weeks of social material, a listenable
credential for proposals, and occasionally a client.

**The two pitchable stories** (pick per show, never pitch both at once):

1. **The career story.** Eleven years as a BA inside BHP, SA Police and
   Services Australia; went independent because AI leverage means one
   person who understands the business can now deliver what used to need
   a team. Honest about what AI does and does not change. This fits
   business, freelancing and AI shows.
2. **The vertical story.** "Why your [ServiceM8/Cliniko/Shopify] and your
   accounting system disagree, and what it costs you." This fits trade,
   practice-management and industry shows, and each pitch links the
   matching systems guide as proof you can go deep for an hour.

**Target map, in pitch order** (verify each before pitching, per the note
at the top):

- *Vertical trade and industry shows first.* Australian trades-business
  podcasts (The Site Shed has historically been the flagship of this
  niche), bookkeeping and accounting-tech shows, allied-health practice
  management shows, VET-sector and RTO podcasts. Smaller audiences,
  perfect audiences: every listener runs the systems you wrote guides
  about. Find them by searching the podcast apps for the system names
  and "[industry] business podcast australia"; the shows that come up for
  those searches are the ones whose audiences search the same way.
- *Australian small-business shows second.* The established
  small-business marketing and operations podcasts (Small Business Big
  Marketing is the long-running archetype). Bigger audiences, broader
  fit; pitch the career story with one vertical anecdote.
- *Freelance, indie and dev shows third.* Freelancing and
  indie-consulting podcasts, .NET and web-dev shows for the Linehaul and
  AI-methods story. These build peer authority (Tier 2 fuel) more than
  client leads.
- *Local media, opportunistically.* Adelaide and SA business coverage
  (InDaily's business desk and similar SA outlets) when there is an
  actual story: a launch, a milestone, an SA-relevant angle on AI and
  small business.

**The mechanics that make it work:**

- Pitch like a professional: the templates section has the email; it is
  short, names a specific episode, offers two specific topics, links your
  strongest proof, and asks for nothing else.
- Start with smaller shows and record every appearance link on a running
  list; each yes makes the next pitch easier, and three good episodes
  justify a `/media/` page on the site (a small build I can do the day it
  is warranted: bio, headshot, topics, past appearances, decent-mic
  proof).
- Sound matters more than video: a quiet room and a reasonable USB mic
  are the entire kit. Listen to one of your own recordings before
  pitching anyone; everyone hates this step and it fixes more than any
  other prep.
- After each episode: thank the host with a specific note, repost with
  two sentences in your voice (the SEO-PLAN LinkedIn habit), add the
  episode to the running list, and suggest one other show and guest to
  the host if you genuinely can. Podcast hosts all know each other;
  being a generous guest is the referral loop.
- Reciprocity is fine when honest: some hosts appreciate you sharing the
  episode with your audience; paid placement dressed as guesting is not
  fine and reads exactly like what it is.

## Tier 5: guest writing and reactive PR (steady drips)

- **Reactive source requests.** SourceBottle is the Australian-native
  service where journalists request expert sources; international
  equivalents (Qwoted and successors to the old HARO model) cover global
  outlets. Fifteen minutes scanning per week; respond only where you are
  genuinely the right expert, with three tight sentences and a
  credential. One landed quote in a real outlet is a high-authority link
  and a permanent "as featured in".
- **Industry association newsletters and blogs.** Every industry page you
  wrote has associations behind it: trade associations, bookkeeper
  networks, VET peak bodies, practice-owner groups. Their newsletters
  are hungry for practical, non-salesy expert content, and your systems
  guides are already the outline. One adapted piece per quarter, pitched
  to the body whose members it serves.
- **Guest posts, selectively.** Only on sites a real buyer or referrer
  reads (a bookkeeping practice's blog, an agency's resources section, an
  established dev publication), always original, never paid-placement
  farms. If a site advertises "write for us" pricing, it is selling
  links, Google knows, walk away.
- **The data study, when the material accrues.** Original data is the
  most linkable content that exists. Within a year the calculator's
  anonymised topic lines, the reconciliation war stories, or a systematic
  read of hundreds of marketplace job posts ("what Australian small
  businesses actually ask developers for") can become one substantial
  annual piece that every roundup in the niche cites. Flagging it now so
  the data gets kept; I can do the analysis and the write-up when you
  say go.

## Tier 6: local Adelaide (the disproportionate lane)

Local queries are winnable in months, not years, and local authority
compounds fastest of all because the pond is small:

- **Meetups and conferences.** Adelaide has an active tech scene: .NET
  and web-dev user groups, AI meetups, and DDD Adelaide as the flagship
  community conference. Attend first, talk later: a lightning talk on
  "running a one-person AI-leveraged practice" or the $3.8m
  reconciliation story is exactly conference-shaped. Speaker listings
  are links; the hallway is Tier 2.
- **Business SA and local business networks.** Membership organisations
  with directories, events and newsletters. Judge by the room, not the
  brochure: one visit before any membership fee.
- **University and TAFE guest slots.** Flinders, Adelaide, UniSA and TAFE
  SA course coordinators want industry guests, especially on AI-era
  practice. An hour of honest talk, an .edu.au-adjacent listing, and the
  students are future colleagues (Tier 2, again).
- **SA media and awards.** InDaily, CityMag and the SA business press
  cover local operators with a story; SA's various business awards (40
  Under 40 and sector awards) are applications, not luck, and finalists
  get links and a line for the site. Apply when there is a genuine
  result to point at, not before.
- **Sponsorships, honestly sized.** A local club or community event
  sponsorship that you would do anyway as a community member sometimes
  carries a website link. Do it for the community reason or not at all;
  as a link-buying scheme it is both ineffective and transparent.

## The never list

Symmetry with SEO-PLAN.md's red lines, because off-page is where most
operators quietly go grey:

- No bought links, no link farms, no PBNs, no Fiverr backlink packages.
  All of it is detectable, most of it is wasted money before it is a
  risk.
- No reciprocal link-exchange schemes ("you link me, I link you" at
  scale). Natural mutual links between real partners are fine; schemes
  are a pattern Google names in its spam policies.
- No guest posts on pay-to-play farms, no article spinning across
  directories.
- No fake reviews, ever, anywhere, in either direction. One discovered
  fake costs more than fifty real reviews earn. The review-ask template
  in OUTREACH.md is the entire strategy.
- No pretending to be a journalist's perfect source when you are not;
  SourceBottle credibility is single-use if burned.
- No automation of any of this. Mass-personalised outreach reads as what
  it is, and every tier above works precisely because the volume is low
  and the sincerity is real.

## Cadence

**Weekly (the two hours, same day each week):**
- 30 min: one Tier 2 touch. A bookkeeper coffee email, an agency intro,
  a testimonial offered, a warm reply. One, done properly.
- 30 min: ecosystem presence (Tier 1). Answer one or two real questions
  in one platform community.
- 15 min: SourceBottle and equivalents scan; respond only on genuine fit.
- 45 min: the current campaign (whatever this fortnight's push is: a
  podcast pitch batch, a launch prep, a directory sweep, an association
  pitch).

**The first 90 days, in order:**
1. Weeks 1 to 2: Tier 0 complete (it is a checklist; finish it), the
   LinkedIn announcement post to the professional network.
2. Weeks 3 to 6: Tier 1 applications submitted (platform partner
   directories, with systems guides as proof); first five bookkeeper
   emails; first testimonials offered to tools you rate.
3. Weeks 7 to 10: first podcast pitch batch (five vertical shows, one
   story each); first tool launch (pick the one with the best story) on
   HN and the directories.
4. Weeks 11 to 13: first association-newsletter pitch; attend two local
   events cold, no agenda beyond the room; review what the listening
   posts and Search Console links report show.

**Quarterly:** re-run the numbers below, retire what produced nothing,
double what produced anything, and pitch one talk.

## Measurement

Off-page is slower and noisier than on-page; measure it or it becomes
superstition:

- **The relationship ledger.** One flat file or sheet: who, tier, last
  touch, what came of it. Ten seconds after each touch. This is the
  actual asset this plan builds, and the only way to know in month six
  which hour was worth it.
- **Links:** Search Console's Links report plus Ahrefs Webmaster Tools,
  checked monthly, expecting single digits per month and caring about
  who, not how many.
- **Referrals:** ask every enquiry how they found you, write it down.
  The contact form should keep asking; so should you on calls.
- **Podcast/PR:** the running appearances list, and referral traffic
  spikes in the days after each episode.
- **The honest quarterly question:** which single channel produced actual
  conversations with actual buyers? Feed that one. This plan lists many
  doors so you can find the two or three that open for you specifically,
  not so you can knock on all of them forever.

## Templates

Same register as OUTREACH.md: short, specific, no theatre. Personalise
the bracketed parts or send nothing.

**The bookkeeper/accountant coffee email:**

> Subject: The [ServiceM8/Shopify/etc] cleanups your clients bring you
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'm a developer in Adelaide who fixes the messes that land on
> bookkeepers' desks: ServiceM8 minting duplicate Xero contacts, Shopify
> payouts that never reconcile, the client who records payments in both
> systems. I've written plain-words guides to the common ones:
> [link to the most relevant systems guide].
>
> I'm not after your work; I'm after the jobs you don't want, the ones
> that are more software than bookkeeping. If a coffee (or fifteen
> minutes on a call) is worth it to work out whether we're useful to
> each other, name a time. If not, keep the guides; they're written to
> hand to clients.
>
> Aaron

**The podcast pitch:**

> Subject: Guest pitch: [specific topic] ([your episode with X] was why
> I picked your show)
>
> Hi [host name],
>
> I listened to [specific episode] and [one genuine, specific sentence
> about it]. I think I can give your listeners an episode in the same
> vein.
>
> I spent eleven years as a business analyst (BHP, SA Police, federal
> government) and now run a one-person AI-leveraged dev practice from
> Adelaide. Two topics I can go deep on for an hour:
>
> 1. [Topic shaped to their audience, e.g. "Why your job-management

>    costs, and the three habits that fix most of it"]
> 2. [Second topic, e.g. "What AI actually changes for a business your

> Aaron

>
> Hi [name],
>

> bearing in it: [one specific, true sentence, e.g. "it's carried every
> client build I've shipped this year"]. If a short customer story or
> quote is useful for your site, I'm happy to give you one with a real
> number in it, attributed to Aaron Steele with a link to [domain].
>

>
> Aaron

**The source-request response formula (SourceBottle and kin):**
Three sentences, in this order: the direct answer to their question with
a specific number or example; the credential in one clause (eleven years
as a BA across mining, policing and federal government, now running an
AI-leveraged dev practice); availability and contact. Nothing else. Send
within hours of the call-out, not days; these close fast.
