# 2. Decide

The most expensive website mistakes happen before anything is built, so
this document happens before anything is built. Work through it with a
notebook; by the end you will have a one-page description of your site
that documents 3 to 5 execute.

## 2.1 What is this site for?

Write down the one job the site must do. Not five jobs: the one that,
if it happened more often, would make the site worth having. For almost
every small business it is one of these:

- **Be findable and credible.** Someone hears your name, searches it,
  and needs to land somewhere that confirms you are real, good and
  contactable. (Most trades, most consultants, most local services.)
- **Generate enquiries from search.** People who have never heard of
  you search for what you do and should find you. (Harder, slower and
  the kit is honest about it in document 5.)
- **Take bookings or payments.** The site is a till or a diary.
- **Sell products.** That is an online store, which is this kit's
  method plus a platform decision; the kit flags where store-specific
  choices diverge.

Everything else (the blog, the gallery, the mission statement) is
decoration around the one job. Decoration is allowed; it just never
gets to delay the job.

## 2.2 The five pages that are almost always enough

A small-business site that does its job is usually five pages, and
"we'll need about fifteen pages" is usually a sign nobody wrote the one
job down:

1. **Home:** who you are, what you do, who for, one clear action
   (call, book, enquire). A visitor should get it in five seconds.
2. **Services** (one page, or one per major service if they are truly
   different audiences): what you do in the customer's words, not your
   industry's.
3. **About:** the human evidence. Real name, real face if you are
   willing, the story in three paragraphs. Buyers of small businesses
   are buying a person; show them one.
4. **Proof:** reviews, past work, photos of real jobs. If you have
   none yet, a smaller honest version beats fabricated polish, and
   document 5 says how to collect proof from day one.
5. **Contact:** every way to reach you, a simple form, your service
   area, hours. No captcha mazes.

## 2.3 What it should cost, so nobody can lie to you

Money, per year, for the DIY route this kit teaches: a domain
(typically $15 to $40 a year), hosting (typically $60 to $300 a year
depending on track, document 3 has specifics), email on your own domain
(typically $0 to $150 a year), and an AI assistant subscription if you
do not already pay for one. Call it **$150 to $500 a year all-in**.

Hours, once: **15 to 40 hours** spread over a few weekends, weighted
towards words and photos, not technology. That surprises people; it is
also the reason document 5 exists.

For calibration, the market you are not buying from: freelancers
typically charge a few thousand dollars for the same five pages,
agencies start where freelancers finish. Those prices are not a scam;
they buy judgement, accountability and your 30 hours back. The kit's
bet is that with the decisions pre-made and the prompts supplied, your
30 hours are enough.

## 2.4 When DIY is the wrong call, before you spend a weekend

Honest list. DIY is probably wrong if any of these is true:

- The site must **replace revenue-critical booking or sales flows** on
  a deadline (a broken checkout costs more than a developer does).
- You need to **move an existing site that already ranks well** on
  search: rebuild-without-losing-rankings is genuinely technical, and
  getting it wrong quietly costs you the phone calls you did not know
  the old site was earning. (My site has a whole page on this; it is
  the one job I most often tell DIYers not to do.)
- The site must **integrate with other systems** (your job-management
  tool, your CRM, member logins). That is software work wearing a
  website costume.
- You have genuinely **no hours to give it**. A half-finished DIY site
  is worse for credibility than no site.

If one of those is you, the kit still was not wasted: documents 2 and
5 make you the best-prepared client any developer ever had, and the
credit line in document 1 stands.

## 2.5 Write the one-pager

Before moving on, write (or dictate to your AI assistant, document 4
shows the method) a single page containing: the one job; your five
pages with two sentences each about what is on them; the single action
you want visitors to take; three sites in your industry you think work
and one you think does not, with a sentence on why. That page is the
specification. Every time a decision gets hard later, it is the
tiebreaker, and if you ever do hire someone, it is the brief.
