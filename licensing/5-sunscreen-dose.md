# 5. One-pump-per-zone sunscreen

**One line:** "One pump per body part. The full SPF on the label, every time."

**Score: 9/14.** Pain 2, workaround 1, five-second 2, fits 2, protectable 0, tailwind 2, access 0. Borderline pass, and the weakest of the five.

## The problem

- SPF is tested at 2 mg of sunscreen per square centimetre of skin.
  People actually apply about half that: one study measured 1.04
  ([Skin Research and Technology, 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/srt.13320)),
  and a review found 0.39 to 1.0
  ([Petersen, 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phpp.12099)).
  Half the amount gives far less than the labelled protection.
- Giving people a strict application guide didn't fix it (same 2023
  study).
- The dispenser does change behaviour. Children applied 0.75 with a
  pump, 0.57 with a squeeze bottle and 0.22 with a roll-on
  ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22250190/?dopt=Abstract)).
- I found no dispenser calibrated to deliver the right dose per pump.
- Trust is low in Australia. In June 2025 CHOICE reported that 16 of 20
  sunscreens it tested fell short of their SPF claims
  ([CHOICE](https://www.choice.com.au/health-and-body/beauty-and-personal-care/skin-care-and-cosmetics/articles/sunscreen-test),
  [ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-06-12/australian-sunscreens-fail-test-spf-claims-choice-report/105394190)).
  The brands disputed the results. A brand that can say "you'll get the
  SPF you paid for" has something to sell.

## The product

A pump pack where one full press delivers one teaspoon (5 mL), with a
body map on the label.

- Seven zones, one pump each: face and neck, left arm, right arm, left
  leg, right leg, front, back. That's the 35 mL "seven teaspoons" adult
  dose that sun-safety advice already uses (from my own knowledge, not
  this search).
- The label shows the seven zones as a simple picture.
- A smaller pump for children's packs.
- For tubes: a nozzle that squeezes a ribbon of fixed width, and a line
  printed on the tube: "a strip this long per zone".

## Prior art found

| Reference | What it is | Why it matters |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| Ordinary lotion pumps | Dispense a small fixed amount, usually well under 5 mL (my knowledge) | The hardware exists. Larger-dose pumps are sold for sauces and syrups |
| The "teaspoon rule" | Public-health guidance | The dose itself can't be claimed |

**There is little to patent here.** The parts and the rule both exist.
What can be protected is a registered design for the label and pump,
and a brand name. A sunscreen company could do this without us. The
reason to pitch anyway is speed: the idea arrives finished, with the
evidence attached, at the moment brands need a trust story.

## Draft description for a provisional

*A sunscreen dispensing package comprising a container, a dispensing
mechanism delivering a predetermined single dose of 4 to 6 mL per
actuation, and indicia on the package dividing the body into zones and
instructing one actuation per zone, the dose and number of zones being
chosen so that the total applied approximates 2 mg/cm² on an adult.*

Variants to include:

- Child-size dose and zone map.
- A ribbon-forming nozzle with a printed length gauge.
- A counter ring on the pump collar that clicks through the seven zones.

Expect an examiner to push back on obviousness. File only if a patent
attorney thinks the counter ring or ribbon gauge is worth claiming.

## Bench test (about A$30 to A$50, my estimate)

**Buy:** a 5 mL-per-press pump (sold for syrups and sauces), a 1 litre
sunscreen pump pack, kitchen scales.

**Do:**

1. Fit the pump. Weigh ten presses to confirm about 5 g each.
2. Print the seven-zone body map and stick it on.
3. Ask ten people to apply sunscreen to one arm from a normal bottle,
   weighing the bottle before and after. Then repeat on the other arm
   with one pump from yours.
4. Record the two averages. That comparison is the pitch.

**It fails if** people find 5 mL per limb too much and wipe it off.

## Sell sheet copy

- **Headline:** One pump. One body part. Full protection.
- **Picture:** the pack with its body map; a simple bar chart of "what
  people apply" against "what SPF needs".
- **Bullets:** Delivers the tested dose. No measuring. Works with any
  formula. Sells more sunscreen per customer.
- **Footer:** Design registration pending. Contact details.

## Risks

- Almost nothing stops a brand doing this alone.
- Sunscreens are regulated as therapeutic goods in Australia. A changed
  pump shouldn't alter the formula's approval, but the label claim
  needs the brand's regulatory team (my understanding; unverified).
- Treat this as a quick, low-cost pitch. Don't spend attorney money on it.
